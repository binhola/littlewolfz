## From Rutherfold to Form Factor
### What we want to learn?
We want to measure the internal structure of particles like protons. Since we can not see them, we shoot electrons at them and see how they scatter. The pattern of scattering tells us the internal charge distribution.

### Rutherfold scattering: classical case
**Set up**: Alpha particles (helium nuclei) hitting gold atoms.
**Key Result**: The Rutherford formula for scattering off a **point charge**:
$$
\dfrac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \propto \dfrac{1}{\sin^4(\theta/2)}
$$
**What it taught us**: Atoms have tiny, dense nuclei (disproving the "plum pudding" model).
**Limitation**: Treats the nucleus as a **point particle** with no internal structure.

#### Momentum transfer in elastic electron scattering
We have:
- Initial electron 4-momentum: $p^\mu = (E, \vec{p})$
- Final electron 4-momentum: $p'^\mu = (E', \vec{p'})$
- Target proton (at rest): $P^\mu = (M, 0)$
- The exchanged virtual photon carries the momentum transfer.
##### Step 1: Define the 4-Momentum Transfer
The momentum carried by the virtual photon is:
$$
q = p - p'
$$
##### Step 2: Calculate $q^2$ (invariant momentum transfer)
In relativity, the "length" of this 4-vector is:
$$
q^2 = p^2 - 2p\cdot p' + p'^2 = 2m_e^2 - 2(E E' - \vec{p} \cdot \vec{p'})
$$
Since electrons are relativistic $E, E' \gg m_e$. So we can approx $m_e \approx 0$:
$$
q^2 \approx -2 p \cdot p' = - 2(E E' - |\vec{p}| \cdot |\vec{p'}| \cos \theta) = -2 E E' (1 - \cos \theta)
$$
We then define
$$
Q^2 = -q^2 = 2 E E' (1 - \cos \theta) > 0
$$
#### **Electron-Proton Scattering: Probing Deeper**
When we scatter electrons off protons, we need a quantum relativistic treatment.
##### **Step 1: The Scattering Matrix (S-Matrix)**
- Describes the probability amplitude for a scattering process
- For electron scattering: $$S_{fi} = \langle f| i \rangle$$
- In first order perturbation theory:
$$
S_{fi} \propto \int V(x) e^{i q \cdot x} d^3 x
$$
where $q$ is the momentum transfer.
##### **Step 2: Introducing the Form Factor**
If the target has extended structure, we define the **Form Factor**:
$$
F(q^2) = \int \rho(r) e^{iq\cdot r} d^3 r
$$
- $\rho(r)$ is charge density distribution
- $F(q^2)$ is Fourier transform of the charge distribution
**Physical meaning**:
- $F(0) = 1$ (total charge normalized)
- As $q^2$ increases, $F(q^2)$ decreases $\to$ probe smaller distance scales
- To resolve a structure of size $R$, you need $q \gtrsim \dfrac{\hbar}{R}$ 
##### **Step 3: The Modified Cross Section**
The cross section becomes:
$$
\dfrac{d\sigma}{d \Omega} = \underbrace{\left( \dfrac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \right)_{\rm point}}_{\text{cross section of point-like particles}} \times \underbrace{|F(q^2)|^2}_{\text{correction due to finite size}}
$$
#### **Measuring the Proton Size**
##### **The Charge Radius**
For small $Q^2$, we can expand
$$
F(Q^2) \approx 1 - \dfrac{1}{6} Q^2 \langle r^2 \rangle + ...
$$
where $\langle r^2 \rangle$ is the mean square charge radius.
The **proton charge radius** is found to be:
$$
r_p \approx 0.84-0.88 \, \rm fm
$$
##### **The Proton Radius Puzzle**
Different measurement methods give slightly different values:
- **Electron scattering**: ~0.88 fm
- **Muonic hydrogen spectroscopy**: ~0.84 fm
This discrepancy is an ongoing research topic.

#### **Rosenbluth Formula: The Complete Picture**
For protons (which have spin and magnetic moment), we need two form factors:

| Form Factor | What it Measures             |
| ----------- | ---------------------------- |
| $G_E(q²)$   | Electric charge distribution |
| $G_M(q²)$   | Magnetic moment distribution |
The **Rosenbluth formula** gives the complete cross section:
$$
\dfrac{d\sigma}{d \Omega} = \left( \dfrac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \right)_{\rm point} \times \left[ \dfrac{G_E^2(q^2) + \tau G_M^2(q^2)}{1 + \tau} + 2\tau \tan^2(\theta/2)G_M^2(q^2) \right]
$$
where $\tau = \dfrac{-q^2}{4M^2}$ 
**Key feature:** By measuring at different angle $\theta$ with the same $q^2$ , we can separate $G_E$ and $G_M$

#### **What We Learn From Form Factors**
**Experimental finding**: Proton form factors follow a "dipole form":
$$
G_E(q^2) \approx \dfrac{1}{\left(1 + \dfrac{q^2}{0.71 \, \rm GeV^2}\right)^2}
$$
**This tells us**:
- The proton has **finite size** (~0.8 fm)
	- **Evidence**: The form factor $F(q^2)$ decreases with increasing $q^2$.
	- **If proton were point-like**: $F(q^2)=1$ (constant) at all $q^2$
	**Quantitative measurement:**
	From the expansion $F\left(q^2\right) \approx 1-\frac{1}{6} q^2\left\langle r^2\right\rangle+\cdots$
	The slope at $q^2=0$ gives the radius:
	$$
	\left\langle r^2\right\rangle=-\left.6 \frac{d F\left(Q^2\right)}{d Q^2}\right|_{Q^2=0}
	$$

	Experimental result: $r_p \approx 0.84-0.88 \mathrm{fm}$
- The charge distribution is **exponential**: $\rho(r) \propto e^{-mr}$
	**Mathematical fact:**
	The Fourier transform of an exponential distribution $\rho(r)=\rho_0 e^{-m r}$ gives a dipole form factor:
	$$
	F\left(q^2\right) \propto \frac{1}{\left(1+q^2 / m^2\right)^2}
	$$
	
	Perfect match: The experimental dipole form with $m^2=0.71 \mathrm{GeV}^2$ corresponds to:
$$
\rho(r) \propto e^{-r / 0.8 \mathrm{fm}}
$$
- The proton is **not elementary** but has internal structure
	**Multiple lines of evidence**:
	1. **Finite size itself**: Elementary particles are point-like
	2. **Magnetic moment**: Proton magnetic moment ≠ 2 (unlike elementary Dirac particle)
	3. **Excited states**: Proton has resonances (Δ⁺, etc.) - only composite objects have excited states
	4. **Deep-inelastic scattering**: At high $q^2$, electrons scatter off **point-like constituents** inside the proton (quarks!)
	5. **Parton distribution functions**: We can measure momentum distributions of quarks inside

**The clincher**: When we shoot high-energy electrons at protons, they sometimes bounce back as if they hit something **hard and point-like** inside - these are the **quarks**.
#### **Deep Questions**
1. **Is the electromagnetic size the same as the strong force size?**
    - Probably similar, but not exactly the same
2. **Is the mass distribution the same as charge distribution?**
    - Unknown - this relates to "gravitational form factors"
3. **Why does this matter?**
    - Important for understanding neutron stars
    - Affects equation of state of dense matter
    - Relevant for quark-gluon plasma studies
### Summary
- **Elastic scattering** measures internal structure via **form factors**
- **Form factors** are Fourier transforms of charge distributions
- **Rosenbluth formula** separates electric and magnetic distributions
- **Proton radius** ≈ 0.85 fm, with an ongoing "puzzle"
- This foundation led to the discovery of quarks via deep-inelastic scattering
